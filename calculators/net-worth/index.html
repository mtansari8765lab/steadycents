How this calculator works

Net worth is what you own minus what you owe. This calculator adds up the assets you enter — cash and bank accounts, retirement and other investment accounts, your home, your vehicles and anything else of value — then subtracts the liabilities you enter: your mortgage, car loan, credit card balances, student loans and other debts. Use current values throughout: for a home or vehicle, enter what it is worth today, not what you paid for it, and for each debt enter the balance you owe now. The debt-to-asset ratio divides your total liabilities by your total assets, so it shows how much of everything you own is matched by debt. If your total assets are 0, the ratio is shown as —, because there is nothing to divide by.

Worked example: cash and bank accounts of $12,000, retirement accounts of $45,000, other investments of $8,000, a home worth $240,000, a vehicle worth $15,000 and $2,000 of other assets come to $322,000 in total assets. Against that sit a mortgage balance of $195,000, a car loan of $9,000, credit card balances of $3,500, student loans of $22,000 and $1,500 of other debts — $231,000 in total liabilities. So $322,000 − $231,000 = a net worth of $91,000, and the debt-to-asset ratio is 71.7% ($231,000 ÷ $322,000). This example is an illustration built from round figures so the arithmetic is easy to check — it is not a picture of a typical household.

Frequently asked questions

What is net worth?
Net worth is what you own minus what you owe. Add up the current value of your assets — cash and bank accounts, retirement and other investment accounts, your home, your vehicles and anything else of value — then subtract everything you still owe, such as a mortgage, a car loan, credit card balances and student loans. The result is a single number that snapshots where you stand today.
Is a negative net worth bad?
Not necessarily, and it is not a judgment of you. A negative net worth simply means the balances you owe currently total more than the current value of what you own. It can happen, for example, soon after taking on student loans or a first mortgage, before years of payments have brought those balances down. What matters most is the direction over time: a net worth that rises as balances fall and savings build is moving the right way, and recalculating regularly shows whether it is heading where you want.
Should I use my home's purchase price or its current value?
Use its current value — a realistic estimate of what the home could sell for today — not the price you paid for it. Net worth is a snapshot of today, so every item should be valued as it stands now, and the same applies to vehicles and other assets: use a current resale estimate rather than the purchase price. If you are unsure, choose the more cautious estimate: an inflated value flatters the result without changing your real position.
How often should I recalculate my net worth?
Every three to six months is a practical rhythm for most households. That is often enough for saving and debt payments to show up as real movement, without chasing short-term swings in home values or account balances. It also helps to recalculate after a major change — paying off a loan in full, buying or selling a home, or a large change in savings — and to use the same categories each time, so each new snapshot is comparable with the last.
Is net worth the same as wealth, and is this financial advice?
No to both. Net worth is a single snapshot number — what you own minus what you owe today — while wealth in everyday life also rests on things no calculator can count, such as your future income, health, skills and time. And this calculator is educational only, not financial advice: it works solely from the values you enter, excludes anything you have not entered, and knows nothing about your taxes, goals or circumstances. For decisions about your own situation, consider a licensed financial adviser or a nonprofit counseling service.

Assumptions & limitations: the figures are a point-in-time snapshot built only from the values you enter. Home and vehicle values change over time and are estimates — use current values, not purchase prices. Debt balances are what you owe now, not the total of future payments. How the maths works

Net worth is assets minus liabilities: everything you own, minus everything you owe. The calculator totals six asset groups (cash, retirement accounts, investments, home value, vehicle value, other assets) and five liability groups (mortgage, car loan, credit cards, student loans, other debts), then reports the difference and a debt-to-asset ratio — total liabilities divided by total assets.

Worked example

Assets: $5,000 cash, $40,000 in retirement accounts, and a home worth $300,000 — $345,000 in total. Liabilities: a $220,000 mortgage and $3,000 on credit cards — $223,000 in total. Net worth is $122,000, and the debt-to-asset ratio is $223,000 / $345,000 = 64.6%. A positive net worth means you own more than you owe; the ratio shows how much of what you own is financed by debt.

Questions

Can net worth be negative?

Yes, and it often is early in adult life — student loans and a car loan can outweigh a small amount of savings. A negative number is a starting point, not a verdict; what matters is the direction it moves as debts fall and savings grow.

Should I count my home at its full value?

Yes, but use a realistic value, not the purchase price or an optimistic guess — and remember the mortgage sits on the other side of the same calculation. Counting the home without the mortgage would overstate the result.
