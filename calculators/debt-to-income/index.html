How this calculator works

A debt-to-income ratio compares the debt payments you owe each month with the income you earn each month, shown as a percent. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) defines DTI as all your monthly debt payments divided by your gross monthly income — the amount you earn before taxes and other deductions are taken out. Lenders use it as one way to measure your ability to manage monthly payments and repay money you plan to borrow. This calculator works out the two versions lenders look at. Front-end DTI counts only your housing payment: housing ÷ gross monthly income. Back-end DTI counts housing plus every other recurring monthly debt: total monthly debt ÷ gross monthly income. Both use gross income, not take-home pay — using take-home pay would make the ratios look larger than the ones a lender calculates.

What counts is a contractual debt payment: your housing payment (mortgage or rent, including taxes, insurance and HOA where they apply), the minimum payments on credit cards (the minimums, not the balances), auto loans, student loans, personal loans, child support and alimony. What does not count is ordinary spending that is not a debt payment: utilities, groceries, subscriptions, savings and other day-to-day costs are left out, however large they are, because they are not payments you owe under a credit agreement or court order.

The 28/36 guideline — a guideline, not a law

The best-known yardstick is the 28/36 rule: housing at or below 28% of gross monthly income, and total monthly debt at or below 36%. It is a long-used guideline, not a law — no statute requires your ratios to sit at or below those figures, and many households sit above one or both, especially where housing is expensive. This calculator turns the percentages into dollars at your income so the comparison is concrete: at $6,500 a month, the 28% housing guideline is $1,820 and the 36% total-debt guideline is $2,340.

What happened to the 43% figure?

The 43% figure has a specific history. It was the CFPB's original cap for a General Qualified Mortgage: under the original General QM definition, a loan's total monthly debt-to-income ratio could not exceed 43%. The CFPB's 2020 General QM rule replaced that cap with price-based tests that compare the loan's APR with the average prime offer rate (APOR) for a comparable transaction. The rule took effect on March 1, 2021, with mandatory compliance from October 1, 2022. So 43% is best treated as a historical benchmark, not a limit: it no longer operates as a federal cap on General QM loans.

That does not mean DTI stopped mattering. Lenders still weigh DTI when they underwrite a loan, and each lender, investor or loan program sets its own ceiling, which may sit above or below 43%. For example, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac allow back-end ratios up to 45–50% with automated underwriting approval and strong compensating factors, while FHA's standard sits around 43%. Your lender's ceiling for your loan program — not any single famous percentage — is the figure that decides an application.

Worked example: gross monthly income of $6,500 and a housing payment of $1,800 give a front-end DTI of $1,800 ÷ $6,500 = 27.7%. The other debts — credit card minimums of $150, an auto loan of $350, student loans of $300 and $70 of other payments — come to $150 + $350 + $300 + $70 = $870, so total monthly debt is $1,800 + $870 = $2,670 and back-end DTI is $2,670 ÷ $6,500 = 41.1%. Against the guidelines: the 28% housing guideline is $1,820, so housing sits $20 under it; the 36% total-debt guideline is $2,340, so total debt sits $330 over it; and the 43% historical mark is $2,795, leaving $125 of room. In plain language: housing is within the 28% guideline, while total debt is above the 36% guideline but within the historical 43% mark. This example is an illustration built from round figures so every number is easy to check — it is not a picture of a typical household.

DTI is the check a lender runs when qualifying a mortgage, so if you are testing a home purchase, pair this page with the Mortgage Payment Calculator to see the housing payment itself. Frequently asked questions

What is a debt-to-income ratio?
Your debt-to-income ratio (DTI) is all your monthly debt payments divided by your gross monthly income — your pay before taxes and other deductions — shown as a percent. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau defines DTI this way, and lenders use it as one way to measure your ability to manage monthly payments and repay money you plan to borrow. Different loan products and different lenders set different DTI limits.
What is the difference between front-end and back-end DTI?
Front-end DTI counts only your monthly housing payment — mortgage or rent, including taxes, insurance and HOA where they apply — divided by your gross monthly income. Back-end DTI counts housing plus every other recurring monthly debt, including credit card minimum payments, auto and student loans, personal loans, child support and alimony, divided by the same gross monthly income. Lenders look at both: the front-end ratio shows the weight of housing alone, and the back-end ratio shows the weight of all debt together.
What is the 28/36 rule?
The 28/36 rule is a long-used guideline, not a law. It suggests keeping your housing payment at or below 28% of your gross monthly income and your total monthly debt payments at or below 36%. This calculator turns those percentages into dollar amounts at your income — 28% and 36% of what you earn — so you can compare them directly with the payments you enter. Many households sit above one or both figures, especially where housing is expensive, and lenders do not all use 28% and 36% as cut-offs.
Is 43% still the limit for DTI?
No, not as a federal rule any more. The 43% figure was the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's original cap for a General Qualified Mortgage. The CFPB's 2020 General QM rule replaced that cap with price-based tests that compare the loan's APR with the average prime offer rate, effective March 1, 2021, with mandatory compliance from October 1, 2022. Treat 43% as a historical benchmark, not a limit. Lenders still weigh DTI, and each lender, investor or loan program sets its own ceiling, which may sit above or below 43%.
How can I lower my DTI?
There are only two levers: lower the monthly debt payments or raise gross income. Paying down a balance lowers its minimum payment and can lower the total, paying a small debt off in full removes its payment entirely, and avoiding new recurring debts keeps the total from rising again. On the income side, lenders count documented gross income, so extra earnings only help once they are regular and verifiable. Because DTI uses gross income, cutting non-debt spending such as utilities or subscriptions does not change the ratio itself, though it can free up cash to pay debts down.

How the maths works

Front-end DTI is monthly housing costs divided by gross monthly income. Back-end DTI is all monthly debt payments (housing, cards, auto, student and other) divided by the same gross income. The calculator compares the results with the long-used 28% (housing) and 36% (total debt) guidelines, and with the historical 43% mark. The 28/36 figures are guidelines, not laws, and 43% is a historical benchmark, not a current federal limit — each lender sets its own ceiling.

Worked example

Gross monthly income $6,000. Housing $1,500, credit cards $200, auto loan $350, student loan $150, other debts $0 — total debt payments $2,200. Front-end DTI is $1,500 / $6,000 = 25.0%, inside the 28% guideline. Back-end DTI is $2,200 / $6,000 = 36.7%, just above the 36% guideline. The picture is a household whose housing is affordable but whose total debt load is at the edge of the usual comfort zone — the card and auto payments are what push it over.

Questions

Why gross income, not take-home pay?

Because lenders use gross income when they calculate DTI — it is their standard, from the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's explanation of debt-to-income ratios. Using take-home pay would make the ratio look worse than a lender's version of the same numbers.

Does DTI include everyday spending?

No. Only debt payments count: housing, cards, auto, student and other loan payments. Groceries, utilities and subscriptions are not debts, so they do not appear in either ratio — which is also why a good DTI does not by itself mean the budget balances.
