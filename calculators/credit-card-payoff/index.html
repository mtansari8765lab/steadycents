How this calculator works

It works through your balance month by month. Each month, interest is charged at your APR divided by 12 on the balance you still owe, then your fixed payment is subtracted. Because the payment never shrinks, more and more of it reaches the balance itself as the interest portion falls — the opposite of a minimum payment, which gets smaller as you succeed. The simulation stops when the balance reaches zero; the final month's payment is smaller, covering only what is left plus that month's interest.

Worked example: a $5,000 balance at 22.99% APR with a fixed payment of $200 a month is paid off in 35 months, with $1,871.08 in total interest and $6,871.08 paid in total. Pay only the minimum instead (using the illustrative formula below) and the same balance takes 229 months and costs $8,339.53 in interest — so the fixed payment saves $6,468.46 in interest and clears the card 194 months sooner.

The minimum-payment comparison

The comparison uses one clearly labelled illustrative formula: each month, the payment is that month's interest plus 1% of the balance after that interest is added, with a $25 floor. Real issuers use different minimum-payment formulas — some use a different percentage, some add fees, and some charge a flat percentage of the balance — so this comparison is illustrative, not a quote from any card issuer. Your own card's formula is in its cardholder agreement. What every shrinking minimum shares is the shape of the problem: the payment falls as the balance falls, so the payoff stretches out for years.

Frequently asked questions

How does this calculator work?
It applies your fixed monthly payment to your balance month by month. Each month, interest is charged at your APR divided by 12 on the balance you still owe, then your payment is subtracted. It repeats until the balance reaches zero and reports the number of months, the total interest, the total you paid and the month you become debt-free.
How is the minimum-payment comparison calculated?
The comparison uses one illustrative formula: each month, the payment is that month's interest plus 1% of the balance after that interest is added, with a $25 floor. Real card issuers use different minimum-payment formulas — some use a different percentage, some add fees, and some charge a flat percentage of the balance — so treat this comparison as an illustration of how minimum payments behave, not as a quote from your card issuer.
Why does paying only the minimum take so much longer?
Because the minimum payment falls as your balance falls. At first, most of it is interest plus a small slice of the balance, and as the balance shrinks the payment shrinks with it, so progress slows. A fixed payment stays the same every month, so more and more of it reaches the balance itself — which is why the fixed payment clears the same debt years sooner and for far less interest.
What if my fixed payment does not cover the monthly interest?
Then the balance will not fall. If your payment is less than or equal to the first month's interest, everything you pay is swallowed by interest and the amount you owe stays the same or grows, so the calculator flags it instead of showing a payoff date. To make progress, the payment must be higher than the monthly interest — on the default example, the first month's interest is about $95.79.

No. It is an educational estimate based only on the numbers you enter and the assumptions shown on this page — a constant APR, no new purchases, no fees or penalty rates, and payments at month end. Your card's real figures will differ, because issuers calculate interest on average daily balances. How the maths works

The calculator simulates the card month by month: interest of balance × APR/12 is added, then your fixed payment is subtracted, until the balance reaches zero. It also shows an illustrative minimum-payment comparison. That comparison uses a common formula — each month's interest plus 1% of the balance after interest, with a $25 floor — because real issuers compute minimums differently; it is an illustration, not your issuer's exact rule.

Worked example

Balance $3,000, APR 24%, fixed payment $150 a month. The card clears in 26 months with $869.62 of interest, $3,869.62 paid in total. Paying only the illustrative minimum instead takes 181 months — just over 15 years — and costs $4,801.71 in interest, more than the original balance. That gap is why minimum payments are the expensive way to clear a card.

Questions

Why is the minimum-payment figure only illustrative?

Issuers use different minimum formulas (for example, a percentage of the balance, or interest plus a fixed amount, with different floors). The calculator's formula — that month's interest plus 1% of the balance, minimum $25 — is a transparent stand-in so the comparison is honest about what it is.

What if my payment does not cover the monthly interest?

Then the balance never falls, and the calculator says so plainly instead of inventing a payoff date. The first month's interest on $3,000 at 24% APR is $60; any payment at or below that cannot reduce what you owe.
