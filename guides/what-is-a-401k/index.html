What Is a 401(k)? A Plain-English Guide for 2026

Published November 10, 2026 · Last reviewed November 10, 2026 · Muhammad Talha Ansari

If you have a job in the United States, a 401(k) is probably the biggest retirement tool available to you — and the one most likely to come with free money attached, in the form of an employer match. Yet the name explains nothing, the rules fill an IRS web page, and the choices (how much to put in, traditional or Roth, what to do when you change jobs) arrive all at once, usually in your first week of work. This guide walks through it in plain English: what a 401(k) is, the 2026 contribution limits, how the match and vesting work, what it costs to take money out early, and your four options when you leave a job. Every limit and rule below comes from the Internal Revenue Service (IRS).

What a 401(k) is

A 401(k) is an employer-sponsored defined-contribution retirement plan, named after section 401(k) of the Internal Revenue Code — the part of the tax code that created it. "Defined contribution" means the amount that goes in is what is defined: you choose a percentage of each paycheck to contribute, your employer may add money on top, and the balance you end up with depends on how much went in and how the investments inside the account performed. There is no promised monthly pension at the end; the account, and its balance, are yours.

The money moves straight from your pay into the plan before it reaches your bank account, and it grows inside the account without being taxed each year — no tax on the growth while it stays there. What you buy inside the plan is a separate choice: a 401(k) is a container, and leaving the contributions sitting in whatever the plan holds by default is a decision too. How that growth compounds over decades is the subject of our Compound Interest, Explained Simply guide.

Two ownership facts are worth fixing in your mind from the start. Your own contributions are always yours — they vest immediately, in full, whatever happens. Employer matching money may take time to vest, which means it becomes fully yours on a schedule rather than all at once; vesting is covered in the match section below.

Traditional or Roth 401(k): when do you pay the tax?

Many plans offer two versions of the same account, and — exactly as with IRAs — the difference is a single question: when do you pay the tax? Some plans offer only the traditional version, so check which yours has.

What makes a withdrawal qualifiedTaken in retirement, per the plan's rulesAge 59½ and the 5-year rule both met
Required withdrawalsRequired minimum distributions from age 73 or 75 (see below)None — eliminated from 2024

The same reasoning as the IRA choice applies: a traditional 401(k) tends to fit if you expect a lower tax rate in retirement than today, and a Roth 401(k) tends to fit if you expect a higher one. The 2026 contribution limits

These are the IRS limits for 2026, announced in Notice 2025-67 and news release IR-2025-111. The headline figure — how much of your own pay you can put in — rose from $23,500 in 2025:

2026 limitFigure
Employee elective deferral (your own contributions)$24,500
Catch-up contribution, age 50 or older$8,000
Your total at age 50 or older$32,500
Catch-up contribution, ages 60–63 (SECURE 2.0)$11,250
Your total at ages 60–63$35,750
Total annual additions — employee plus employer, excluding catch-ups$72,000
Total annual additions including the standard catch-up$80,000
Total annual additions at ages 60–63$83,250
Compensation that may be taken into account (cap)$360,000
  • The $24,500 limit is per person, not per plan. It covers your combined elective deferrals across all plans you participate in during the year. Change jobs mid-year and the deferrals at your old employer count toward the same $24,500 — the limit does not restart.
  • Employer money does not use up your $24,500. Matching and other employer contributions count toward the $72,000 annual-additions total, not toward your own deferral limit.
  • Your plan may set a lower limit. The figures above are federal maximums; a plan is allowed to cap contributions below them.
  • Excess deferrals have a deadline. If you go over the $24,500 across plans, notify the plan administrator before April 15 of the following year so the excess, adjusted for earnings, can be distributed back to you. Miss that step and the excess can be taxed twice — once when it went in, and again when it eventually comes out.
  • High earners: catch-ups must be Roth from 2026. If your prior-year FICA wages from the employer sponsoring the plan exceeded $150,000, any catch-up contributions you make must be designated as Roth — after-tax — rather than pre-tax.

The employer match — and vesting

A match is extra money your employer adds to your account because you contributed, usually calculated as a percentage of what you put in, up to a cap expressed as a percentage of your pay. Formulas vary from employer to employer; two common examples — examples, not universals — are:

  • 100% of the first 3% of pay you contribute, plus 50% of the next 2% — contribute 5% of pay and the employer adds 4%.
  • 50% of the first 6% of pay you contribute — contribute 6% of pay and the employer adds 3%.

Read your own plan's formula and find the contribution level that earns the full match. Contributing less than that level is the closest thing in personal finance to turning down part of your pay: the match is compensation the employer has already set aside for you, available only if you contribute enough to claim it. That is why capturing the full match usually comes before extra debt payments or IRA contributions in any sensible order of operations.

Vesting decides when the employer's money becomes fully yours. Your own contributions are always 100% vested from day one. Employer contributions may vest all at once after a set period (cliff vesting) or gradually, year by year (graded vesting). Federal rules cap how long these schedules can run, but within those caps plans vary — so if you are thinking of changing jobs, check your plan's summary plan description for its exact schedule and your current vested percentage before you hand in notice. Leaving shortly before a vesting milestone can mean leaving real money behind.

Illustrative example only

Example 1 — what the match is worth. Take a salary of $60,000 and a contribution of 6% of pay: $3,600 a year from the employee. The employer matches 50% of the first 6% of pay, adding $1,800 a year. Together that is $5,400 a year going in.

Let that run for 30 years at a steady 6% a year, with contributions made at the end of each year. The $5,400 a year becomes $426,914.21 — of which $162,000 is contributions and $264,914.21 is growth. Without the match, the employee's $3,600 a year alone becomes $284,609.47. In this example the match — money the employee never had to earn twice — is worth $142,304.74 of the final balance.

These figures are invented to make the arithmetic easy to check. They assume a constant 6% return every year, no fees, no raises and no inflation — real returns vary year to year, and real salaries and limits change. Run your own numbers with the Retirement Savings Calculator.

Why starting early matters so much

A 401(k) rewards time more than it rewards large contributions, because growth compounds: each year's growth itself grows in the years after. The second example isolates that effect — same monthly amount, same return, only the start date changes.

Illustrative example only

Example 2 — the ten-year head start. Save $200 a month at a steady 7% a year, with contributions at the end of each month. Starting at age 25 and stopping at 65 — 40 years — the account reaches $524,962.68, on $96,000 contributed. Starting at age 35 instead — 30 years — it reaches $243,994.20, on $72,000 contributed.

The ten extra years add only $24,000 of contributions, yet more than double the outcome — the difference is a decade of extra growth on growth, not a decade of extra saving. Same assumptions as before: a constant 7% return every year, no fees, no raises and no inflation. Real returns vary, which is exactly why the habit of starting matters more than the habit of timing.

Taking money out before age 59½

A 401(k) is built to be hard to raid, and the tax code enforces that. The general rule: a withdrawal from pre-tax money before age 59½ is taxed as ordinary income, plus a 10% additional tax on top. Plan the account as retirement money, because that is how it is priced. There are exceptions and alternatives, and they are narrower than most people assume:

  • The rule of 55. If you leave your job in or after the calendar year you turn 55, withdrawals from that employer's plan can escape the 10% additional tax (income tax still applies). It applies only to the plan of the employer you just left — not to IRAs, and not to an old employer's plan you left years earlier.
  • A hardship withdrawal is not a penalty exception. If your plan allows one, it can get money out for an immediate and heavy financial need — but it does not waive the 10% additional tax, and a hardship withdrawal cannot be rolled over into another retirement account. The growth those dollars would have made is gone for good.
  • A 401(k) loan is a separate option. If your plan allows loans, you borrow from your own balance and repay it with interest back into the account. It avoids the tax and the 10% additional tax while it is repaid — but it must actually be repaid. If you leave the job with a balance outstanding and do not repay it, the unpaid balance can be treated as a taxable distribution, with the 10% additional tax on top if you are under 59½.

Required minimum distributions (RMDs)

Pre-tax 401(k) money cannot stay sheltered forever. Under SECURE 2.0, required minimum distributions from a traditional 401(k) must start at age 73 if you were born from 1951 to 1959, and at age 75 if you were born in 1960 or later. Each year you must withdraw at least the required amount, and it is taxed as ordinary income as it comes out.

Two more RMD facts are worth knowing. First, SECURE 2.0 eliminated RMDs from Roth 401(k) accounts starting in 2024 — designated Roth money in a workplace plan no longer has to come out during the original owner's lifetime. Second, missing an RMD is expensive: the excise tax is 25% of the shortfall — the amount that should have come out but did not — reduced to 10% if the shortfall is corrected within two years and Form 5329 is filed.

When you leave a job: your four options

Changing jobs does not end the account — but the money needs a decision from you, because doing nothing is also a choice. For the balance you are vested in, the four standard options are:

  1. Leave the money in the old plan. It stays invested and keeps growing tax-deferred, but you can no longer add to it, and you will have one more old account to keep track of with every job change.
  2. Roll it over directly to an IRA. A direct rollover moves the money trustee-to-trustee, with no tax withheld and no penalty — and opens the account choice up beyond one employer's menu. Our Roth IRA vs. Traditional IRA guide covers the accounts it could land in.
  3. Roll it into your new employer's plan. If the new plan accepts rollovers, this keeps all of your workplace retirement money in one place, under the new plan's rules.
  4. Cash it out. Almost always the expensive option: before age 59½ it usually means income tax plus the 10% additional tax, and — the quietest cost — the money stops growing. A balance cashed out at 35 is not just taxed; it loses thirty years of compounding.

One wrinkle: a plan may automatically cash out or roll over a small balance after you leave, under its own rules. If your balance is small, do not assume it will simply wait for you — check the plan's rules promptly after you leave, so the money ends up where you chose, not where a default put it.

Common traps

  • Contributing, but not enough to earn the full match. Any contribution below the level that earns the whole match leaves part of your pay unclaimed. Find the formula, contribute at least to the full-match level, then decide what to do with the next dollar.
  • Treating the $24,500 limit as per job. It is per person, across all plans, for the year. Two employers in one year means one shared limit — and an excess to fix if the two together overshoot it.
  • Missing the April 15 fix for excess deferrals. An excess that is not distributed, with its earnings, by April 15 of the following year can be taxed twice. If you changed jobs mid-year, add up both plans' deferrals early enough to act.
  • Assuming a hardship withdrawal avoids the 10% additional tax. It does not — hardship is a reason a plan may let money out, not an exception to the additional tax — and the amount cannot be rolled back later.
  • Leaving a job with a loan outstanding. An unpaid 401(k) loan balance after you leave can become a taxable distribution. Check the repayment terms before you resign, not after.
  • Cashing out a small balance because it looks insignificant. Small balances are exactly the ones with the longest time to compound. A direct rollover costs a form; cashing out costs the tax, the 10% additional tax before 59½, and every year of growth.
  • Resigning just before a vesting milestone. Unvested employer money stays behind when you leave. A few weeks' difference in your leaving date can be worth a full year of match — check the summary plan description first.

Frequently asked questions

What is a 401(k)?
A 401(k) is an employer-sponsored retirement plan named after section 401(k) of the tax code. You contribute part of your pay before it reaches your bank account, the money grows without being taxed each year, and many employers add matching contributions on top. Your own contributions are always yours; employer matching money may take time to vest.
How much can I contribute to a 401(k) in 2026?
For 2026 you can defer up to $24,500 of your own pay. If you are age 50 or older you can add an $8,000 catch-up, for a total of $32,500, and if you turn 60, 61, 62 or 63 during the year the catch-up is $11,250 instead, for a total of $35,750. The combined employee-plus-employer limit is $72,000 before catch-ups. Your employer's matching contributions do not count against your $24,500, and the $24,500 limit applies across all plans you participate in, not per plan.
What is a 401(k) employer match?
It is extra money your employer adds to your account, usually a percentage of what you contribute up to a cap — for example, 50% of the first 6% of your pay. To get the full match means contributing at least enough to earn all of it; contributing less leaves pay on the table. Matching money may vest over time, so check your plan's vesting schedule.
What happens to my 401(k) when I leave a job?
You can usually leave the money in the old plan, roll it over directly to an IRA, roll it into your new employer's plan, or cash it out. Cashing out before age 59½ usually means income tax plus a 10% additional tax, and the money stops growing. A direct rollover avoids the tax and the penalty, and a plan may automatically cash out or roll over a small balance, so check the plan's rules.
Can I take money out of my 401(k) before age 59½?
Generally yes, but it is costly: withdrawals from pre-tax money are taxed as ordinary income plus a 10% additional tax. Exceptions include leaving your job in or after the year you turn 55 (the rule of 55, which applies to that employer's plan only). A hardship withdrawal is not an exception to the 10% additional tax and cannot be rolled over. A 401(k) loan is a separate option if your plan allows it.
