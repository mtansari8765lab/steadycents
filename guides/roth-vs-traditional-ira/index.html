Roth IRA vs. Traditional IRA: Which One Fits You?

Published November 7, 2026 · Last reviewed November 7, 2026 · Saeed

A Roth IRA and a traditional IRA are not different investments. You can hold the same funds inside either one, and the money grows the same way while it sits there. The difference is a single question: when do you pay the tax? A Roth IRA takes the tax now and lets qualified withdrawals out tax-free later. A traditional IRA may give you a tax break now and taxes the money as it comes out. Everything in this guide — the 2026 limits, the two income tests, the withdrawal rules — follows from that one trade-off, and every figure below comes from the Internal Revenue Service (IRS).

What each account is

Roth IRA. You contribute money you have already paid tax on — there is no deduction for putting it in. In exchange, qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free, and that includes all of the growth, not just what you put in. There are no required withdrawals for the original owner: the money can stay in the account for as long as you live.

Traditional IRA. Contributions may be deductible on your tax return, which lowers this year's tax bill if you qualify. The money then grows without being taxed each year, but withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income. The IRS also requires the money to start coming out: required minimum distributions (RMDs) begin at age 73.

Both accounts are containers, not investments in themselves. What you buy inside the container — and whether you buy anything at all — is a separate choice, covered in the opening steps below. The growth itself works the same way in either account, as our Compound Interest, Explained Simply guide shows.

The 2026 contribution limits

For 2026, the IRS limit rose from $7,000 in 2025. The same limit applies whether you choose Roth, traditional, or a split between the two:

2026 ruleFigure
Contribution limit, under age 50$7,500
Contribution limit, age 50 or older$8,600
Catch-up included in the age-50 figure$1,100
Deadline for 2026 contributionsApril 15, 2027
  • The limit is combined. $7,500 is the most you can put into all of your traditional and Roth IRAs together for 2026 — not $7,500 in each.
  • You need taxable compensation. Your contributions for the year cannot exceed your taxable compensation for the year.
  • Excess contributions cost you. Money put in above the limit is taxed at 6% per year for each year it remains in the account, until it is removed or absorbed by a later year's limit.

The two income tests — they test different things

Each account has an income test, and the most common confusion in this whole topic is treating them as the same test. They are not. The Roth test limits whether you can contribute at all. The traditional test limits only whether you can deduct the contribution — and it only applies if the relevant spouse is covered by a retirement plan at work. Anyone with taxable compensation can contribute to a traditional IRA; the question is only whether the contribution is deductible.

Illustrative example only

Take someone under age 50, in the 22% federal tax bracket, who contributes the full $7,500 for 2026. In a traditional IRA, if the contribution is fully deductible, it saves $1,650.00 this year ($7,500 × 22% = $1,650.00). In a Roth IRA, the same $7,500 costs that $1,650 now — there is no deduction — but the contribution and all of its growth come out tax-free later if the withdrawal is qualified.

Now let that one $7,500 contribution grow at a steady 6% a year for 30 years. It becomes $43,076.18 — the original $7,500 plus $35,576.18 of growth. Withdraw the whole $43,076.18 from a traditional IRA and the tax at 22% is $9,476.76; at 12% it is $5,169.14. Withdraw the same $43,076.18 as a qualified Roth withdrawal and the tax is $0.

This example is invented to make the arithmetic easy to check. It assumes a constant 6% return every year and constant tax rates, and it ignores state tax, fees and inflation — real returns vary, and real tax rates change. The lesson is in the comparison: if your tax rate going in equals your rate coming out, the two accounts end in the same place. The real difference is made by rate changes over your life — and by the fact that Roth growth is never taxed at all. Project your own balance with the Retirement Savings Calculator.

Which one fits you?

A Roth IRA often fits if:

  • You expect your tax rate in retirement to be higher than it is today — paying tax now, at the lower rate, wins.
  • You are early in your career, when your income — and usually your tax rate — is at its lowest.
  • You want tax-free growth and no required withdrawals, so the money can stay invested as long as you choose.
  • You value being able to withdraw your contributions (not the earnings) at any time without tax or penalty.

A traditional IRA often fits if:

  • You expect your tax rate in retirement to be lower than it is today — taking the deduction now, at the higher rate, wins.
  • You need the deduction now — for example, in a high-income year when this year's tax saving matters most.

You do not have to pick only one: the $7,500 limit for 2026 may be split between both accounts in any proportion. And one ordering point comes before either IRA for many savers: if your employer offers a 401(k) match, capturing the full employer match usually comes first — it is additional pay for saving you were going to do anyway — and only then does the Roth-versus-traditional choice decide where the next dollars go. Getting money out

IRA money is meant for retirement, and the tax rules enforce that — with one important Roth exception:

  • Early withdrawals. Withdrawals before age 59½ generally face a 10% additional tax on top of any income tax due, although exceptions exist for specific situations.
  • Roth earnings need two tests. Earnings come out qualified — tax-free — only after you reach age 59½ and meet the 5-year rule. Before that, earnings taken out can be taxed and penalized even though the contributions were already taxed.
  • Roth contributions are different. The contributions themselves — the money you put in, not the earnings — can be withdrawn at any time, tax- and penalty-free, because you already paid tax on them.
  • Traditional withdrawals are taxed. Money taken from a traditional IRA is taxed as ordinary income, and required minimum distributions must start at age 73 whether you need the money or not.
  • Roth owners have no RMDs. There are no required withdrawals for the original owner of a Roth IRA.
  • Conversions are always allowed — and always taxed. You may convert traditional IRA money to a Roth IRA at any income level, but the converted amount is taxed as income in the conversion year. Converting a large balance in one year can push you into a higher tax bracket, so the size and timing of a conversion are part of the decision, not an afterthought.

How to open one

  1. Decide Roth, traditional, or a split. Use the rate question above — higher rate expected later points to Roth, lower rate expected later points to traditional — and check the two income tests for your filing status.
  2. Choose a provider. Banks, credit unions and brokerage firms all offer IRAs. Compare fees and the investments available inside the account, and confirm the account is the type you intended before you fund it.
  3. Fund it before the deadline. For the 2026 tax year, contributions must be made by April 15, 2027 — and tell the provider the contribution is for 2026, so it is counted against the right year's limit.
  4. Choose the investments inside. The IRA is a container: money left as uninvested cash earns little. Decide what the money is invested in as part of opening the account, not as a step you get to later. Our How to Start Investing With Little Money guide covers the usual first choice — one broad, low-cost index fund — and how a small automatic monthly amount gets it started.
  5. Automate it. A regular automatic contribution spreads the $7,500 across the year and removes the monthly decision — the same pay-yourself-first habit as any other savings goal.
  6. Revisit when your income changes. A raise, a career break or a move into a different bracket can flip which account fits — and can move you into or out of the phase-out ranges. Recheck the tests each year you contribute.

Common traps

  • Contributing to a Roth above the income limit. The excess is taxed at 6% per year until it is fixed. Check your modified AGI against the phase-out table before you contribute, especially in a year with a bonus or a one-off gain.
  • Deducting a traditional contribution you are not entitled to deduct. If a workplace plan covers the relevant spouse and your income is above the phase-out, the deduction is not available — claiming it anyway creates a tax problem, not a saving.
  • Forgetting the limit is combined. $7,500 across all of your traditional and Roth IRAs for 2026 — contributing the full amount to each one creates an excess contribution in the second account.
  • Leaving the money uninvested. An IRA holding only cash is a container with nothing working inside it. The account does not invest for you.
  • Converting a large balance in one year. The whole converted amount counts as income that year and can push you into a higher bracket. Splitting a conversion across years is often the calmer route.
  • Treating the IRA as an emergency fund. Retirement money taken out early can mean tax plus a 10% additional tax, and the growth those dollars would have made is gone permanently. Keep a separate emergency fund for emergencies.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a Roth IRA and a traditional IRA?
The difference is when you pay tax. A Roth IRA takes after-tax money in, and qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free, including all growth, with no required withdrawals for the original owner. A traditional IRA may let you deduct contributions now, but withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income, and required minimum distributions start at age 73. You can hold the same investments inside either account.
How much can I contribute to an IRA in 2026?
For 2026 you can contribute up to $7,500 if you are under age 50, or $8,600 at age 50 or older, which includes a $1,100 catch-up. The limit is combined across all of your traditional and Roth IRAs, it cannot exceed your taxable compensation for the year, and the deadline for 2026 contributions is April 15, 2027. The limit rose from $7,000 in 2025, and excess contributions are taxed at 6% per year while they remain in the account.
Can I contribute to a Roth IRA if I earn too much?
It depends on your modified adjusted gross income. For 2026, single and head of household filers can contribute in full below $153,000, a reduced amount from $153,000 to $168,000, and nothing at $168,000 or more. Married couples filing jointly can contribute in full below $242,000, a reduced amount from $242,000 to $252,000, and nothing at $252,000 or more. For married filing separately when you lived with your spouse, the phase-out is $0 to $10,000. Conversions from a traditional IRA to a Roth IRA are allowed at any income, but the converted amount is taxed as income in the conversion year.
Can I have both a Roth IRA and a traditional IRA?
Yes. You can hold both and split your saving between them, but the $7,500 limit for 2026 ($8,600 at age 50 or older) is combined across both accounts, not $7,500 each. Whether a traditional contribution is deductible is a separate test: if neither you nor your spouse is covered by a workplace plan, it is fully deductible at any income, and if the relevant spouse is covered, the deduction phases out over the income ranges explained in this guide.
When can I take money out without a penalty?
Roth IRA contributions can be withdrawn at any time tax- and penalty-free, but earnings are only qualified tax-free after age 59½ and once the 5-year rule is met. Withdrawals before age 59½ generally face a 10% additional tax on top of any income tax due, with some exceptions. Traditional IRA withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income, and required minimum distributions start at age 73, while a Roth IRA has no required withdrawals for the original owner.
