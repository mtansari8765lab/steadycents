How to Build Credit From Scratch (United States)

Published October 20, 2026 · Last reviewed October 20, 2026 · Muhammad Talha Ansari

If you have never borrowed money, there may be nothing in your credit file at all. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau calls people in this position “credit invisible”: lenders cannot see any record of how you handle debt, so they cannot judge the risk of lending to you. That can make renting an apartment, financing a car, or qualifying for a good credit card harder than it needs to be.

A credit score is simply a summary of your reported borrowing and repayment behavior. It is not assigned to you at birth, and it is not a judgment on your character. It is built, month by month, from information lenders and creditors report about you. This guide walks through the legitimate ways to start building that record in the United States, the habits that protect it, and the traps to avoid.

First, understand what a score is made of

The most widely used scoring model, FICO, builds a score (300–850) from five factors, with payment history and amounts owed carrying the most weight:

  • Payment history — whether you pay on time, every time. This is the single biggest factor.
  • Amounts owed — how much of your available credit you are using (called utilization).
  • Length of credit history — how long your accounts have been open.
  • New credit — recent applications and newly opened accounts.
  • Credit mix — having different kinds of accounts (for example, a card plus an installment loan), though this matters less than the factors above.

For a deeper explanation, see our guide Credit Scores Explained (United States). The key takeaway here: a score rewards consistent on-time payments and modest, well-managed use of credit. There is no legitimate shortcut — anyone promising to create a score overnight is selling something else.

Five legitimate ways to start

Start with one of these, not all five. A single well-managed account builds a clean file; several new accounts at once look risky and complicate your life.

  1. Become an authorized user on someone else's card. A trusted family member can add you as an authorized user on their credit card. Their account history — including its age and payment record — then appears on your credit file, which can give your score a head start. The cautions matter as much as the benefit: if they pay late or carry high balances, that damage lands on your file too. Only do this with someone whose habits you trust, and confirm the card issuer reports authorized-user activity to the bureaus.
  2. Open a secured credit card. This is the most common starting point. You put down a refundable security deposit — often a few hundred dollars — and that deposit becomes your credit limit. Use the card for one small recurring expense, set up autopay to pay the statement balance in full each month, and let the issuer report your on-time payments. After roughly 6–12 months of consistent payments, many issuers will upgrade you to an unsecured card and return your deposit. Compare annual fees before you apply; the best secured cards charge none.
  3. Take out a credit-builder loan. Offered by many community banks and credit unions, these work in reverse: the “loan” amount is locked in a savings account while you make monthly payments, and your payments are reported to the credit bureaus. When the term ends, you receive the money (minus any fees or interest). Shop around — fees and interest vary widely — and make sure the lender reports to all three major bureaus (Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion), since a payment that is never reported does nothing for your file.
  4. Get your rent payments reported. Most landlords do not report rent to the bureaus on their own, so your biggest monthly payment usually builds no credit history at all. Rent-reporting services can add it: they verify your payments with your landlord and report them to the bureaus. Before signing up, check three things: which bureaus they report to, what they charge, and whether your landlord will cooperate with verification. This is a useful add-on, not a replacement for a real credit account.
  5. Keep it simple. Whichever path you choose, one account managed perfectly beats three accounts managed carelessly. Open your first account, automate the payment, and give it time.

Habits that protect the score you are building

  • Pay on time, every time. Set up autopay for at least the minimum payment so a forgotten due date never becomes a late mark. Paying the full statement balance avoids interest entirely.
  • Keep utilization low. The commonly cited guideline is to use well under 30% of your credit limit — lower is better. On a $300-limit secured card, that means keeping the reported balance under about $90.
  • Do not apply for several accounts at once. Each application can generate a hard inquiry, and a cluster of new accounts shortens your average account age.
  • Keep your first account open. Length of history helps your score, so that first secured card or builder loan is worth keeping even after you qualify for better products.

Myths and traps to avoid

  • You do not need to carry a balance to build credit. This is the most persistent myth in personal finance. Paying your balance in full each month builds exactly the same payment history as carrying a balance — without the interest charges. Never pay interest just to “build credit.”
  • Credit-repair companies cannot erase accurate history. The Federal Trade Commission warns that companies promising to remove accurate negative information, or offering to create a “new credit identity” for you, are scams. Only inaccurate information can be disputed and removed — and you can dispute errors yourself for free (see our guide How to Check Your Credit Reports Free (United States)).
  • You do not need to pay to see your credit standing. Your free credit reports are available at AnnualCreditReport.com, and many banks and card issuers show customers a free credit score each month.
  • Avoid “no credit check” shortcuts that cost a fortune. High-fee cards and payday-style products marketed to people with no credit can trap you in debt that damages the very file you are trying to build.

How long does it take?

A FICO score generally requires about six months of reported account activity before it can be calculated. From there, steady on-time payments and low utilization can produce meaningful progress within 12 months. Building credit is slow by design — the score is meant to reflect a track record, not a moment. Start now, automate the boring parts, and let time do the rest.

Frequently asked questions

How long until I have a credit score?
A FICO score typically needs around six months of activity on a reported account before it appears. VantageScore models can generate a score sooner, but most lenders use FICO.
Will checking my own credit hurt my score?
No. Checking your own reports or score is a “soft” inquiry and has no effect. Only applications for new credit create hard inquiries.
Do I need to carry a balance to build credit?
No. Paying in full each month builds the same on-time payment history as carrying a balance, without interest charges.
Can my rent payments build credit?
Only if they are reported to the bureaus. Most landlords do not report on their own; rent-reporting services can add this history for a fee.
What is the fastest legitimate way to start?
There is no shortcut, but the standard path is fast enough: a secured credit card or credit-builder loan, one small automated payment, on-time payments every month, and patience.
