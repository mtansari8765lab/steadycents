Fed Decision on October 28: What a Hold or a Quarter-Point Hike Could Mean for Your Savings, Credit Cards, and Mortgage

Published October 20, 2026 · Updated October 28, 2026 · Muhammad Talha Ansari

On Wednesday, October 28, 2026, the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) — the Federal Reserve's rate-setting committee — announced its decision on the target range for the federal funds rate. The committee met October 27–28, the announcement landed at 2:00 p.m. ET, and a press conference followed at 2:30 p.m. ET. This meeting comes with no new Summary of Economic Projections (the "dot plot"), so the decision itself and the Chair's words afterward carry the news.

What the Fed actually decided — October 28, 2026

Update, October 28, 2026: The FOMC decided to maintain the target range for the federal funds rate at 3.75% to 4.00% — a hold, not a hike and not a cut. The statement, released at 2:00 p.m. ET on October 28, 2026, was approved by a 10–2 vote; Lisa D. Cook and Beth M. Hammack voted against the action, preferring to raise the target range by a quarter percentage point at this meeting. That means Scenario A below is what happened: the target range stays where the September decision put it, the prime rate stays at 7.00% on the guide's hold logic, and Scenario B is kept only as an explainer of what a hike would have meant. Source: Federal Reserve, "Federal Reserve issues FOMC statement," October 28, 2026 (federalreserve.gov).

The current target range is 3.75% to 4.00%, set on September 16, 2026, when the FOMC raised it by a quarter point. The published minutes of that September meeting showed most officials expected one more quarter-point increase would likely be appropriate sometime before the end of 2026, with the timing depending on incoming data. That is background, not a forecast for October.

Before the announcement, nobody outside the committee room knew the outcome. This guide did not predict it — it showed what each of the two likely outcomes could mean for your money, so you were ready either way. The decision is now in (see above): the Fed held. The two scenarios below are kept as an explainer. The decision matters beyond Washington because the same target range quietly steers the rate on your savings account, the APR on your credit cards, and the quotes lenders offer on new mortgages — each in a different way, and at a different speed.

How a Fed move reaches your money

The Fed sets one rate directly: the target range for the federal funds rate, the overnight rate banks charge each other. Everything in your wallet connects to it indirectly, through three different channels:

  • The prime rate moves fast. Most variable credit card APRs are set as the prime rate plus a margin, and the prime rate moves in step with the Fed's target range — it stands at 7.00% after the September decision. HELOCs and some other loans are priced off the prime rate too. When the target range moves, these rates typically follow by the same amount, quickly.
  • Savings APYs are each bank's own choice. No rule forces a bank to change the APY on your savings account when the Fed moves. A bank may pass on part of a change, all of it, or none at all — and any change usually arrives over the following weeks, not on the day.
  • Mortgage rates follow markets, not the Fed. Lenders price mortgages off bond markets and their own costs and competition. Those markets often move in the same direction as Fed policy over time, but around any single decision mortgage rates can move by more, by less, or even in the opposite direction.

Scenario A: The Fed holds (target range stays 3.75%–4.00%)

In this scenario, the committee leaves the target range where September put it. A hold is not a cut and not a hike — and for most household rates, its defining feature is that nothing changes automatically.

Savings

Your savings APY stays at whatever your bank has already set. A hold gives banks no new reason to raise or lower deposit rates, so the APY you see today would typically stay the APY you see next month — though a bank can reprice its accounts at any time, for its own reasons, Fed decision or not.

Credit cards

Variable card APRs stay where they are. With the target range unchanged, the prime rate would stay at 7.00%, and a variable APR built as prime plus a margin has nothing new to add. Fixed-rate card offers and promotional rates are set by each issuer and are unaffected either way.

Mortgages

An existing fixed-rate mortgage is unaffected in every scenario — its rate and payment are locked for the term. For new loans, quotes keep following the bond market and lender pricing, exactly as they do today. The average 30-year fixed rate was 7.40% in the week ending October 15, 2026 (15-year: 6.71%), per Freddie Mac's survey — that figure is a market snapshot, not a Fed setting, and it can drift up or down after a hold just as it does in any other week.

Scenario B: The Fed hikes a quarter point (target range becomes 4.00%–4.25%)

In this scenario, the committee raises the target range by a quarter point, to 4.00%–4.25%. What reaches your accounts differs by product — here is each one, with a worked estimate and its assumptions stated.

At least one major bank is publicly positioned for this scenario. In a client note on Monday, October 20, 2026 (reported via Investing.com and IndexBox), Bank of America reiterated its call for an October hike, citing September inflation and projecting that core PCE inflation could plausibly finish 2026 at 3.4% year-over-year; the bank also noted that most FOMC members had backed two hikes during 2026. Polymarket, by contrast, as of October 24 priced an October hold at about 93% (a hike at roughly 7%) — a reminder that one bank's confident call and the market's odds are two different things, and neither is the decision itself.

Final pre-decision odds — Tuesday, October 27, 2026. All three venues below had priced a hold as the likeliest outcome for October 28, with small gaps between them that are normal: each venue asks the question in its own way, with its own traders and update times. Polymarket (seen about 4:12 p.m. PKT, October 27) priced no change at 92.5% and a 25-basis-point increase at 8%. Kalshi's discrete October decision market (seen about 4:13 p.m. PKT, October 27) priced an unchanged rate at 92% and a 25-basis-point hike at 7%. CME FedWatch, which derives its probabilities from federal funds futures (data as of 6:00 a.m. CT, October 27), put no change at 90.6% and a hike at 9.4%. These were pre-decision market prices at those times, not the decision itself — the announcement came Wednesday, October 28 at 2:00 p.m. ET, and the decision was a hold at 3.75%–4.00% (see "What the Fed actually decided" above). The next PCE release follows on Thursday, October 29, after the decision.

Credit cards

The prime rate would typically rise by the same quarter point, to 7.25%, and most variable card APRs would rise with it — usually showing up within one to two billing cycles. Your issuer's margin over prime does not change; only the prime part moves.

Worked example — estimate

Assumptions: a $5,000 balance that stays at $5,000 for a full year, charged at the average APR on credit card accounts assessed interest from the Federal Reserve's G.19 report — 22.15% in Q2 2026. At 22.15%, the interest comes to roughly $1,108 over the year. If a quarter-point hike lifted that APR to 22.40%, the interest would be roughly $1,120 — about $12.50 more per year, or roughly $1 a month. This is a simplified estimate: real cards charge interest on your average daily balance, and payments and new purchases change the real figure.

Savings

A hike raises what banks earn on reserves and short-term lending, but each bank chooses whether — and how much — to pass on to savers. Some raise APYs within weeks; some raise them by less than the Fed's move; some do not move at all.

Worked example — estimate

Assumptions: $10,000 held for a full year in an account paying an example APY of 3.90%. That earns about $390 in a year. If that bank passed on the full quarter point, a 4.15% APY would earn about $415 — roughly $25 more per year. Your bank may pass on some, all, or none of a hike, and may take weeks to do it. To see what different rates do over longer periods, try the compound interest calculator with your own balance and APY.

Mortgages

An existing fixed-rate mortgage payment never changes — a hike does not touch it. Adjustable-rate mortgages are different: they reset off market indexes and could adjust at their next scheduled reset, by an amount set by the index and the loan's caps, not directly by the Fed. For a new loan, the Fed does not set the rate at all — but it is still useful to see what a quarter-point move in market rates would look like, because markets sometimes move roughly with the Fed.

Scenario illustration — estimate

Assumptions: a $400,000, 30-year fixed-rate loan, principal and interest only (no taxes, insurance, or fees). If the average 30-year rate moved up a quarter point from 7.40% to 7.65%, the monthly payment would rise from about $2,769 to about $2,838 — about $69 more per month, or roughly $24,800 more over the full 30 years if the loan ran to term. This illustrates the size of a quarter point; it is not a forecast that mortgage rates would move that way, or at all.

What you can do either way

None of this requires a prediction. The same short checklist leaves you ready for either announcement:

  • Check which of your rates are variable. Look at each card, loan, and savings account and note whether its rate can move, and what it follows. Variable rates are the only ones a Fed decision can reach directly.
  • Know your card APR and when your next statement closes. Your APR is on your statement and in your card's app. If a hike came, a higher APR would typically apply from a coming statement cycle — knowing the date tells you when to look.
  • If you carry a balance, extra payments help at any rate. Every extra dollar paid reduces the balance that interest is charged on, hold or hike. The debt payoff calculator shows what a modest extra payment does to your payoff date and total interest.
  • Compare savings APYs, because banks differ. Whatever the Fed does, banks set APYs independently, and the gap between banks is often larger than a quarter point. The savings goal calculator and the compound interest calculator let you compare what different APYs would earn on your actual balance.
  • If you are mortgage-shopping, get multiple quotes. Lender pricing varies day to day and lender to lender, independently of the Fed. Run each quote through the loan payment calculator to compare monthly payment and total cost on equal terms.

Frequently asked questions

When exactly is the decision?
The FOMC announced its decision on Wednesday, October 28, 2026 at 2:00 p.m. ET, after its October 27–28 meeting: it held the target range at 3.75%–4.00%. A press conference followed at 2:30 p.m. ET. This meeting has no new Summary of Economic Projections (dot plot).
Does a Fed hike change my existing fixed mortgage?
No. A fixed-rate mortgage keeps its rate and payment for the whole term, whatever the Fed does. Only new loans are priced at current market rates.
How soon would my credit card APR change after a hike?
Most variable card APRs are set as the prime rate plus a margin, and the prime rate moves in step with the Fed's target range. If the Fed hiked, most variable APRs would typically rise by the same quarter point within one to two billing cycles.
Will my savings APY definitely rise after a hike?
No. Each bank sets its own APYs and chooses whether to pass on some, all, or none of a hike — and changes can take weeks. A hike makes higher APYs possible, never guaranteed.
Does the Fed set mortgage rates?
No. Mortgage rates follow bond markets and lender pricing. They often move in the same direction as Fed policy over time, but they can move by more, by less, or in the opposite direction around any single decision.
