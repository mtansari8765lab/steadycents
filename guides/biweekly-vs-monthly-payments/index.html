Biweekly vs. Monthly Payments: What Actually Saves Money

Published October 27, 2026 · Last reviewed October 27, 2026 · Muhammad Talha Ansari

"Biweekly" sounds like a small scheduling tweak — and as a schedule, it is. But the reason it saves money is not the calendar. It is arithmetic: paying half your monthly payment every two weeks quietly adds up to one whole extra payment every year, and that extra payment goes straight to principal. This guide explains the mechanism plainly, works a full mortgage example with real numbers, shows three ways to get the same effect without any special program, and lists the traps to check before you hand the job to anyone else.

How biweekly payments work

A biweekly schedule means a half-payment every two weeks. A year has 52 weeks, so that schedule produces 26 half-payments a year — the equivalent of 13 full monthly payments instead of 12. Nothing about the loan itself changes: same balance, same rate, same required monthly payment on paper. The only difference is that by the end of the year you have paid one extra monthly payment, and that extra payment is applied to principal.

Principal is where the saving comes from. Interest is charged each month on the balance you still owe, so every dollar of principal you remove early removes interest on that dollar for every remaining month of the loan. The extra payment also starts a quiet chain reaction: with a slightly lower balance, slightly less of each regular payment is consumed by interest, so slightly more of it reduces principal — month after month, for years. That is the same mechanism behind every extra payment, on any debt: our Debt Payoff Calculator shows it on a single balance, and our snowball vs. avalanche guide shows how to aim extra payments when you have several debts at once.

Not the same as twice a month: a semimonthly schedule — a half-payment twice a month — produces 24 half-payments a year, exactly 12 full payments. No extra payment, no saving. If a schedule does not add up to 13 full payments a year, it is a budgeting convenience, not a payoff strategy.

The worked example: a $300,000 mortgage at 6.5%

Take a $300,000 fixed-rate mortgage at 6.5% over 30 years, principal and interest only (no taxes, insurance or fees). Here is the monthly schedule next to the biweekly one:

MonthlyBiweekly
Payment$1,896.20 once a month$948.10 every two weeks
Payments per year12 full payments26 half-payments (= 13 full)
Total paid per year$22,754.45$24,650.65
Extra to principal each year—$1,896.20 (one full payment)
Total interest over the loan$382,633.47$298,648.95
Loan paid off in360 months (30 years)292 months (24 years, 4 months)
The bottom line

On these terms, the extra payment each year pays the loan off 5 years 8 months early and saves $83,984.52 in interest. The biweekly checks themselves feel smaller — $948.10 instead of $1,896.20 — but the household pays $1,896.20 more per year in total. The saving is real; it is just bought with that extra payment, not conjured up by the calendar.

How this example is modeled, honestly: the figures above are the one-extra-payment-a-year equivalent — the standard monthly schedule plus one extra monthly payment applied to principal each year. An individual lender's true biweekly timing can shift the results slightly: a servicer that credits each half-payment as it arrives gets the principal down a little sooner, while one that holds half-payments (see the traps below) gets it down a little later. The direction and the rough size of the saving are the same either way; the exact payoff month and interest total move a little.

Run your own loan through the Mortgage Payment Calculator: enter your price, down payment, rate and term, then try the extra monthly principal field. In this example, the monthly equivalent of the biweekly schedule is one-twelfth of a payment — about $158.02 a month — so entering that as extra principal shows you nearly the same payoff date and interest saving. For a non-mortgage loan, the Loan Payment Calculator gives you the monthly payment and total interest to start from.

Three ways to get the same effect

The schedule is packaging; the extra principal is the product. All three of these deliver the same extra payment, and all three are equal in effect if the extra money is applied to principal:

  1. A lender-run biweekly program. Your servicer debits half a payment every two weeks — often a natural fit if you are paid biweekly yourself. The convenience is real, but check for an enrollment fee and confirm how partial payments are handled (both traps, below) before signing up.
  2. One extra monthly payment once a year, made by you. Send one extra payment — from a tax refund, a bonus, or a planned savings line — marked "apply to principal." No program, no fee, and you choose the month.
  3. Add one-twelfth extra to each monthly payment. Divide your monthly payment by 12 and add that amount to every payment, again marked for principal. On the example loan that is $158.02 a month. This is the smoothest option for budgeting, because the cost is spread evenly instead of landing once a year.

Traps to check first

  • Third-party biweekly services that charge for what you can do free. Companies exist whose only job is to collect your half-payments and forward them — for a setup fee and a fee on every payment. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau sued one national biweekly company, alleging it charged a setup fee of up to $995 plus a transaction fee with each payment, kept the first extra payment as its fee, and promised savings that a typical customer would need years in the program just to break even on the fees. The Federal Trade Commission's warning about paid debt help points the same way: anyone who charges you upfront, before delivering what they promised, is showing you a scam sign. If a service cannot state its total fees in writing, walk away.
  • Servicers that hold half-payments. Some servicers do not apply a partial payment when it arrives; they hold it until the rest of a full payment accumulates, then apply the whole payment at once. With that arrangement, paying more often saves nothing by itself — only the extra payment helps, and even it arrives later than you sent it. Ask your servicer directly: "Are partial payments applied when received, or held until a full payment is complete?"
  • Extra money counted as a future payment instead of principal. An extra amount can end up treated as next month's payment paid early, which advances your due date but barely touches principal. Confirm — on the payment itself and again on your next statement — that the extra was applied to principal.
  • Prepayment terms in your loan note. Check your note for a prepayment penalty before accelerating any loan. The CFPB notes that prepayment penalties do not normally apply when you pay extra principal in small amounts at a time — but it also advises double-checking with your lender, because the penalty terms are whatever your note says.

When biweekly is not the priority

An extra mortgage payment is a good use of money only after three better uses are covered:

  • Higher-interest debt first. A dollar of credit card balance at 20%+ APR costs roughly three times as much per year as a dollar of this 6.5% mortgage. Pay the minimums on everything, then aim extra money at the highest rate — the order our snowball vs. avalanche guide compares in detail.
  • An emergency fund first. Extra principal is hard to get back: you cannot pay a repair bill with home equity you built last spring. Without a starter cushion, the next surprise goes on a credit card and undoes the plan — see Emergency Fund: How Much Do You Actually Need?
  • An employer retirement match first. If your employer matches retirement contributions, that match is an immediate return on the dollars you contribute — larger than any interest saving an extra mortgage payment can produce. Contribute enough to capture the full match before sending extra principal.

Frequently asked questions

What does biweekly actually mean — twice a month?
No. Biweekly means every two weeks, and there are 52 weeks in a year, so you make 26 half-payments — the equivalent of 13 full monthly payments. Twice a month would be 24 half-payments, exactly 12 full payments, with no extra payment and no saving. The whole biweekly effect comes from those two extra half-payments a year.
Where does the saving actually come from?
From the extra payment going to principal, not from paying more often by itself. Interest is charged on the balance you still owe, so a lower balance means less interest every month that follows. In the worked example, one extra $1,896.20 payment a year cuts total interest by $83,984.52 and shortens the loan by 5 years 8 months.
Do I need a special program or a third-party service?
No. You can create the same effect yourself: make one extra monthly payment once a year, or add one-twelfth of a payment to each monthly payment, and confirm the extra is applied to principal. Be wary of services that charge setup or monthly fees for this — the CFPB has sued a biweekly payment company over false promises of savings, and the FTC warns that anyone who charges upfront before delivering debt help is showing a scam sign.
Will my servicer apply my half-payments as soon as I send them?
Not necessarily. Some servicers hold a partial payment until the rest of a full payment arrives, which delays or removes the benefit. Ask your servicer two questions before relying on a biweekly schedule: are partial payments applied when received or held, and will extra money be applied to principal rather than counted as a future payment? Get the answers in writing.
Should I put extra money toward my mortgage before other debts?
Usually not. Extra money generally saves more against higher-interest debt — a credit card at 20%+ APR costs far more per dollar than a mortgage at 6.5%. A starter emergency fund and any employer retirement match also usually come before extra mortgage principal, because they protect you in ways a lower mortgage balance cannot.
