Renting vs. Buying a Home: A Plain-English Comparison

Published November 14, 2026 · Last reviewed November 14, 2026 · Saeed

Renting versus buying is usually argued with the wrong two numbers: monthly rent against monthly mortgage principal and interest. That comparison leaves out most of what owning actually costs — and most of what renting actually buys you. This guide works through the full cost of each side, with one worked example, and ends with a checklist you can run on your own numbers. No answer here fits everyone: the right choice depends on how long you will stay, how stable your income is, and how much cash you can put down and still keep in reserve.

The true monthly cost of owning

A mortgage calculator usually shows only principal and interest, and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau warns that this can seriously understate what you will actually pay each month. A homeowner's monthly bill is usually PITI — principal, interest, property taxes and homeowners insurance — plus, where they apply, private mortgage insurance (PMI), homeowners-association or condo fees, maintenance and repairs, and utilities a landlord used to cover. A fixed-rate mortgage steadies the principal-and-interest part, but property taxes and insurance premiums can still rise. A common budgeting guideline is to set aside roughly 1% to 2% of the home's value each year for maintenance and unexpected repairs — a guideline, not a guarantee: an old roof or a failed furnace does not check your budget first.

The true upfront cost of buying

The down payment is only the first cheque. Closing costs typically run 2% to 5% of the purchase price, excluding the down payment, according to the CFPB, with the actual figure depending on the loan, the home and the location. Then come moving costs, furnishing, and the emergency cushion that must stay untouched after closing.

Down payment minimums by loan type (these are program minimums, not guarantees that every borrower qualifies at the minimum): conventional loans can start at 3% down for qualifying first-time buyers; FHA loans require 3.5% down with a credit score of 580 or higher; VA and USDA loans require no down payment for eligible borrowers. Twenty percent is not a requirement to buy — it is the threshold that avoids PMI on a conventional loan.

What renting really costs

Renting has costs of its own, and they are simpler to see: the first month's rent, a security deposit (often about one month's rent), renter's insurance (usually inexpensive), and whatever utilities the lease assigns to you. Rent can rise at renewal, and you build no equity from rent payments. In exchange, the landlord covers most maintenance and repairs, and you can move when the lease ends without paying to sell a home. Rent is not "throwing money away": it buys shelter and flexibility without the debt, the maintenance, or the market risk that come with owning.

A worked example: a $300,000 home against $1,800 rent

Illustrative example only

Buy: a $300,000 home, 10% down ($30,000), a $270,000 loan on a 30-year fixed rate at 6.5% — monthly principal and interest $1,706.58. Property tax at 1% of the home's value a year: $250 a month. Homeowners insurance (an example figure, not a quote): $150 a month. PMI at 0.5% of the loan a year: $112.50 a month. Maintenance at 1% of the home's value a year: $250 a month. Total monthly cost of owning: $2,469.08 — $669.08 more per month than the $1,800 rent, before any equity or tax effects.

Upfront: $30,000 down plus $6,000 to $15,000 in closing costs is $36,000 to $45,000, against about $3,600 to start renting (first month plus one month's deposit).

Equity: how it actually builds

Home equity is the difference between the market value of your home and the amount you still owe on it. The CFPB notes that in the first several years of a mortgage you build equity slowly, because early payments are mostly interest. On the worked example's loan, $17,461.15 of the $20,478.96 paid in the first year is interest, and after five years only $17,250.10 of principal has been repaid, leaving a balance of $252,749.90. Equity also depends on the home's value, which can fall as well as rise, and selling a home involves its own transaction costs — so equity is not the same thing as guaranteed profit, and a short stay can leave you worse off than renting.

The tax question, in one paragraph

The mortgage interest deduction lets some homeowners deduct some or all of the mortgage interest they pay — but only if they itemize deductions, as the CFPB explains, and many homeowners take the standard deduction instead. A possible tax break is a small factor in a large decision, not a reason to buy on its own.

The five-question checklist (before you buy)

  1. Will you stay at least about five years? Selling costs eat short stays, so a move you can already see coming argues for renting.
  2. Is your income stable enough to carry the full monthly cost — PITI plus PMI, fees and maintenance — for years, not just this year?
  3. Do you have the down payment and closing costs plus an emergency fund that stays untouched? Work out the fund first with our Emergency Fund guide.
  4. Does the full monthly cost fit your budget? Check your debt-to-income ratio with the Debt-to-Income Ratio Calculator — housing costs up to about 28% of gross monthly income and all debts up to about 36% are the usual guidelines.
  5. Could you still afford the payment if your income dropped for two months? If the answer is no, the house is too expensive, whatever a lender will approve.

When renting wins, and when buying wins

Renting usually wins when:

  • You expect to move within a few years
  • Your income or employment is changing
  • You have not saved enough for the upfront costs
  • You do not want responsibility for maintenance and repairs
  • You value flexibility

Buying usually wins when:

  • You plan to stay put for many years
  • Your income is stable
  • You have the down payment and closing costs plus an emergency fund left over
  • The full monthly cost fits comfortably in your budget
  • You want the stability and control of owning your home

Mistakes to avoid

  • Comparing rent with principal and interest only, and calling the difference a saving.
  • Spending the whole emergency fund on the down payment and closing costs.
  • Waiting years to save 20% while rents rise — 20% is a PMI threshold, not a requirement to buy.
  • Buying for the tax break.
  • Ignoring homeowners-association fees and maintenance when you run the numbers.
  • Stretching to the maximum a lender will approve — a lender's maximum is not the same as affordable.

Frequently asked questions

Is renting throwing money away?
No. Rent buys shelter and flexibility, and it comes without the debt, maintenance and market risk of owning. Owning also has costs renters never pay — interest, taxes, insurance, PMI, maintenance and selling costs — so the honest comparison is the full monthly cost of each side, not rent against a mortgage payment alone.
Do I need a 20% down payment to buy a house?
No. Conventional loans can start at 3% down for qualifying first-time buyers, FHA loans require 3.5% with a credit score of 580 or higher, and VA and USDA loans require no down payment for eligible borrowers. Twenty percent is the threshold that avoids PMI on a conventional loan, not a requirement to buy.
How long should I plan to stay for buying to make sense?
There is no fixed rule, but a longer stay spreads the upfront and selling costs over more years. For a stay under about five years, renting often wins on cost alone — run your own numbers, including closing costs on the way in and selling costs on the way out, before deciding.
What is PMI, and when does it end?
Private mortgage insurance protects the lender — not the homeowner — if a borrower with less than 20% equity stops paying. On a conventional loan you can usually ask to cancel PMI once you reach 20% equity, and the lender must cancel it automatically when the loan balance reaches 78% of the original purchase price. FHA loans use a separate mortgage insurance premium that often stays for the life of the loan when the down payment is under 10%.
My rent keeps rising — should I buy?
Rising rent is a real factor in favour of buying, but compare the full cost of owning — principal and interest, taxes, insurance, PMI, maintenance and fees — against the rent, not rent against principal and interest alone. How long you will stay and how much cash you keep in reserve matter at least as much as the direction of rents.
