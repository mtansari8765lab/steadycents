Debt Snowball vs. Avalanche: Which Pays Off Debt Faster?

Published October 19, 2026 · Last reviewed October 19, 2026 · Saeed

Both methods share the same skeleton: pay the minimum on every debt, then aim every spare dollar at one target debt. They differ only in how the target is chosen — and that one choice changes the interest you pay and how the journey feels.

The two methods

  • Debt snowball: target the smallest balance first, for fast wins. When it's gone, roll its whole payment into the next smallest.
  • Debt avalanche: target the highest interest rate (APR) first. When it's gone, roll its payment into the next highest rate.

The same debts, both ways — a verified example

Four debts, minimums totaling $200/month, plus $150 extra each month — $350 a month while all four debts are still open, falling as each debt is cleared. We simulated both methods month by month (fixed APRs, no new charges):

Here, the avalanche saves $268.60 and three months. But look at the snowball's order: its first two victories arrive on the same debts, almost as fast — because in this example the smallest debt also had the highest rate, the methods agree at the start and diverge later. Try the Snowball vs. Avalanche Calculator with your real debts; the gap may be smaller — or much larger — than the example.

So which should you choose?

  • Choose the avalanche if you want the mathematically cheapest path and numbers motivate you.
  • Choose the snowball if quick account closures are what will keep you going — a plan followed beats a cheaper plan abandoned. The interest difference is often a few hundred dollars; quitting early costs far more.
  • Hybrid: clear one small debt for momentum, then switch to highest-rate order. The math police will not arrest you.

Either way: keep minimums on everything (missed minimums mean fees and credit damage), stop adding new charges to cards you're repaying, and build a starter emergency cushion so the next surprise doesn't undo the plan.

Frequently asked questions

Does the snowball ever beat the avalanche on interest?
No — the avalanche is mathematically optimal for interest, given the same payments. When both methods target the same debt first (as in the example above), early results can look identical.
What if a minimum payment doesn't cover the monthly interest?
Then that balance grows despite paying. This happens with very low minimums on high-APR debt; the fix is directing extra money there regardless of method, or speaking to the lender or a nonprofit credit counselor about options.
